What changed in 1.1

Version 1.0 described a 10% commission on the notary's fee as what the code did, and proposed restructuring it. That commission has since been removed from the product, not merely proposed for removal: Nota now charges the client its own published price, per service, and the notary's honoraires reach them whole. Every economic figure here has been recomputed from the shipped pricing grid. Gross margin per act is materially lower than version 1.0 claimed — about 66%, not 89–91% — because Nota bears the card-processing fee on the whole amount the client pays, including the notary's fee, which Nota never keeps.

01

What Nota is, and why now

A two-sided marketplace where the axis of price is the date, not the document.

A client posts the date they need an acte notarié signed and the amount they offer. Notaries watch a public calendar — the carnet — and pick up the work that fits their schedule. Because every offer is attached to a date, a signature needed tomorrow clears at a multiple of one needed in three weeks. That is the entire mechanism, and nothing in Québec does it today.

Two things make this fundable now rather than in five years.

The product already exists

A pure business-rules core, an HTTP and DynamoDB API, a zero-dependency web app, Stripe payments, notary authentication, an admin console, transactional email, a Cucumber BDD suite and Terraform infrastructure live on AWS in ca-central-1. Built solo. This raise does not fund a build — it funds distribution and liquidity.

The rails for a remote act are law

Bill 34, in force 24 October 2023, made the acte notarié technologique permanent in Québec. Remote signature is legal — but under art. 46 it is exceptional, party-requested and circumstance-justified. That constraint is not an obstacle to route around. It is the wedge.

The ask. $250,000 for twelve months, to take the live product from zero to proven liquidity in the Québec City market, restructure monetization onto déontologie-safe footing, and build the compliance layer that makes remote signing routine for the cases where the law already allows it.

02

Two findings that reshaped this plan

Written against the statute and the code, not against assumptions. Both came back different from the working hypothesis, and both are load-bearing.

Remote signing is already legal — and that is better news than it sounds

The original Phase 2 premise was that clients must appear in person, and that going fully online expands the market a hundredfold. In Québec that premise is half wrong, and the half that is wrong is the half that was going to be expensive.

The acte notarié technologique signed by videoconference is permanent law, not a pandemic measure. The infrastructure exists and is sanctioned: a prescribed videoconference channel, the notary's official digital signature, a technological minute. Nota does not need to lobby a remote act into existence, invent a legal instrument, or wait on a regulator. That is years and hundreds of thousands of dollars removed from Phase 2.

What is true is narrower and more interesting. Under art. 46 of the Loi sur le notariat, a notary may only exceptionally authorize a remote signature. The request must come from a party, and the justifying circumstance must be specific to that party — distance from an available notary, health or functional limitation, weather, an unforeseen event prejudicing another party. Convenience does not qualify.

The actual barrier

It is not the technology, and not the law's silence. The justification burden and the disciplinary exposure sit entirely on the individual notary, who therefore defaults to "come in person" — the only option that never generates a complaint.

That is a product. Nota captures the party's request in their own words at intake, classifies the qualifying circumstance, collects the supporting evidence, and attaches a timestamped, auditable justification record to the dossier. The notary receives a file that is already defensible. A discretionary judgment call with personal downside becomes a standard, evidenced workflow — and this is worth more to a notary than the marketplace itself.

The expansion does not come from making a legal signature possible. It comes from removing geography as a matching constraint.

The revenue model was a déontologie risk. It has been removed.

Version 1.0 of this plan reported that the billing layer implemented a 10% commission on a completed act, collected against the notary's account, and proposed restructuring it. A later variant made the percentage float between 5% and 15% according to an internal score. Both are gone from the product. They are stated here only so a reader who finds them in the git history or in a dated audit knows they were retired, and by which decision.

As implemented, the notary received 90% of their own professional fee and a non-notaire kept 10% — the textbook shape of prohibited fee-sharing, exposing every notary on the platform to a disciplinary complaint. It was the single largest structural risk in the business, and it was removed before the first live act rather than after.

No share of any professional fee. Both lines read before committing.

An offer carries two lines, which the client reads separately before committing anything: the honoraires, which reach the notary whole, and the price of Nota — a grid published in advance, set by the service asked for plus a date-guarantee line, and never by the notary, their record, or the value of the act. The card is authorized for the total on Nota's own Stripe account; at signing Nota captures that total, keeps its own two lines and transfers the honoraires out. Nota deducts nothing from a professional fee, and the notary abandons nothing.

Why this shape and no other

Four texts, read against the code, forbid the retired arrangement and require this one.

  • Art. 32.1 2° of the Loi sur le notariat presumes usurpation of a notary's functions by an intermediary who "obtains from a notary the abandonment of part of their fees" — 2,500 to 125,000 $, doubled on repeat.
  • Art. 32 of the Code de déontologie forbids a notary from sharing fees with someone who is not a member of a professional order. The same conclusion taken from the other end: the prohibition binds the notary, the presumption binds Nota, and fixing one without the other fixes nothing.
  • Art. 29.1 forbids any agreement endangering the notary's independence and disinterest — which a fee indexed on a score awarded by a private company is.
  • Art. 32.1 3° excludes the intermediary who supplies services with no responsibility toward the notary for their fees. Nota authorizes, captures and guarantees the notary's net, deliberately.

No notary was ever billed under the retired model — no act had yet been carried on the platform when it was removed.

What is still open

The direction of the money is settled and verifiable on the Stripe wire: the client pays the platform. What remains open is the legal qualification of Nota's own price, plus three narrower questions — the display of evaluations (art. 70), the qualification of the internal cote, and price presentation (art. 71–72, including the fact that taxes and disbursements are in neither line and appear nowhere in the product yet).

Budgeted

$20,000 of this round buys a formal déontologie opinion and structured engagement with the Chambre des notaires before the first live act. Getting an opinion on file is also a competitive asset: it is the kind of work a well-funded entrant from outside Québec will not know it needs to do.

Wording discipline, permanently

No surface, document or comment may describe Nota as taking a commission, a share or a split of a notary's fees; may attach a rating, average or cote to a named notary on a client surface (art. 70); may claim to be cheaper than a notary (art. 32.1 1°); or may call Nota's price fixed — it is a grid, published per service.

03

Two sides, both stuck

Neither can find the other, and neither can price the thing that actually varies.

The client

  • Price discovery costs a sequence of phone calls during business hours.
  • Published ranges are wide — a mortgage or refinancing act runs roughly 1,500–3,500 $ — and you learn where you land only after you call.
  • Urgency has no price. There is no way to pay to jump a three-week queue, because there is no queue and no market.
  • And the deadline is not yours. A mortgage rate hold expires on a date the lender set; when the notarial calendar cannot meet it, you lose the rate — thousands of dollars over the term, caused by an appointment nobody could price.

The notary

  • A hole in the calendar is unrecoverable inventory — it expires like an airline seat.
  • No channel exists to sell short-notice availability. A cancelled Thursday cannot be listed anywhere.
  • Client acquisition is referral and signage.
  • Buying leads is ineffective; sharing fees is prohibited.

A deregulated market with real price dispersion, real supply elasticity and real demand urgency — and no mechanism connecting any of it.

04

The mechanism

The tier is derived from how many days away the signing date is. It is the axis that makes the calendar mean something.

standard15+ days 1.0×
rapide8–14 days ≈2×
prioritaire2–7 days ≈3×
urgence1 day ≈3.5×
extremesame day ≈4×

Bar length is the tier's mid-band premium against the 5× ceiling. This ladder prices the notary's fee — art. 49 4° of the Code de déontologie lets a notary weigh the degree of urgency in setting fees — and it is not a constant: the domain shrinks the observed median premium of retained offers toward the published band, so the ladder learns from the market. Nota's own date-guarantee line is separate and published beside it: 0 $ · 50 $ · 100 $ · 200 $ · 300 $. Two numbers, two justifications, two lines on the quote — never one number doing both jobs.

Guardrails

Offers are floored at a prix de départ per service and hard-capped at five times it, so the market is a real auction rather than a race to the bottom or a panic tax. Both the browser and the server load the same rules module, so the price a client sees and the price the server enforces cannot drift. Anonymity is default-on and enforced server-side, not merely in the interface.

Two services, both financing, chosen for a bounded intake

Each launch service has a short document checklist a layperson can complete alone, in one sitting, without a professional pre-consult.

Retired

Testament and procuration were retired. They were the wrong first market for a marketplace that sells a date: a will has no external deadline, so urgency is a preference rather than a cost, and at a 650 $ or 295 $ act value Nota's own price would have weighed 30% or more of the transaction. A financing act arrives with a rate hold that expires — a deadline the client did not choose and cannot move.

Acte de vente stays excluded: it requires coordinated document transfer between broker, surveyor and lender before the notary can begin. That is a workflow product, not a web form. Discipline about what not to launch is why the marketplace can be self-serve on day one. Each service also carries a dynamic base price built from criteria collected during intake — a loan above 600,000 $ adds 350 $ and above 1 M$ adds 600 $, a private lender adds 300 $, a declared urgency signed entirely online adds 400 $ — so the client gets an accurate price and the notary gets a pre-qualified file with a complexity weighting already computed. Note which side of the invoice these move: they are criteria on the notary's fee, not on Nota's price, which depends only on the service and the date.

Why the architecture is a commercial fact

The domain core depends on nothing; the API and the web app adapt it to a transport and a user interface. Business rules exist in exactly one place. Adding a fourth service, a new pricing criterion or a new jurisdiction is a data change in the domain package, not a rewrite — Phase 2 and Phase 3 are cheap because Phase 1 was built correctly. Law 25 compliance is designed in rather than bolted on: Canadian data residency, anonymity default-on, consent at the point of collection.

The honest consequence of the financing-first pivot: the addressable act count is roughly a third of the retired three-service plan (~110,000 against ~300,000), because wills were the volume and they are gone. Year 3's 11,000 acts are about 10% of that volume, and the plan says so rather than keeping the old denominator.

Beyond the two services

The acte de vente rides those same ~90,000 residential transactions, at 1,500–3,500 $ each, arriving on the same lender deadline and through the same referral channel. Wills and mandates remain a real ~200,000-a-year category to return to once liquidity exists, on a pricing basis that suits a 650 $ act. Beyond Québec sits the civil-law notariat: the International Union of Notaries spans roughly ninety member states, including France, Belgium, Switzerland and most of Latin America, all running the same instrument Nota is built around. That is where a hundredfold claim actually lives — not in avoiding a car trip.

Why now

  1. Remote acts became lawBill 34 made the technological notarial act permanent in October 2023. The rails exist and are sanctioned. Nota is not betting on legislation.
  2. Thirty-five years of blind pricingFees free since 1991 with no discovery mechanism ever built. The gap does not close on its own — someone has to build the venue.
  3. The renewal wallRoughly 1.2 million Canadian fixed-rate mortgages renewing in 2025 — and every one arrives with a rate hold that expires on a date the client did not choose. That is the demand a time-priced market exists to serve, and it is why the catalogue is financing-first.
  4. A competitor has validated the structureNotairo has been selling its own client-side intake fee in Québec since late 2025 — the same legal shape Nota uses. The structure is no longer novel to explain; what is still unbuilt is publishing the price of the date before the client commits.
  5. Access-to-justice pressureNotary coverage is uneven across the regions — and distance from an available notary is a statutorily recognised ground for signing remotely. The exception categories map onto the under-served.

The one metric that matters

Fill rate — the share of posted bids a notary retains. Below roughly 40% the market is not clearing, and the answer is more supply or better floors. Above roughly 60% the flywheel is real and the constraint moves to demand. Everything else is secondary.

The asset Phase 1 produces is not revenue. It is the only dataset of realised price × days-to-date × act type in Québec — and nobody can reconstruct it without running the same marketplace for the same year.

08

Phase 2 — the act without the trip

Removing geography as a matching constraint.

Today

A client in Québec City is matched against ~400 notaries.

With remote matching

The same client is matched against ~3,900 — and a notary in Rimouski sells an empty Thursday to a client in Gatineau.

The market does not get ten times bigger because the client saves a car trip. It gets ten times more liquid because supply and demand stop being partitioned by postal code. In a marketplace, liquidity is the product.

  1. The exception layer — the wedgeCapture the party's request at intake, classify the qualifying circumstance, collect the evidence, attach a timestamped audit record to the dossier. The notary receives a file that is already defensible. This makes Nota valuable to a notary independent of the marketplace.
  2. Dossier automationPresigned document upload, automated identity and completeness checks, structured hand-off into the notary's existing tooling. The dossier arrives complete or it does not arrive — the difference between selling a lead and selling a file.
  3. Signing railsIntegration with the sanctioned videoconference channel and the notary's official digital signature, so the act is executed and the technological minute closed without leaving the workflow.
  4. Province-wide matchingOnce the above holds, the carnet stops being a Québec City calendar and becomes a Québec calendar.
  5. Cross-border corridorThe statute contemplates a signature received by a notary qualified in a UINL member state, within that state's territory — a legal path to serving Québécois abroad, and the first step onto the civil-law world's rails.
The honest regulatory position

Phase 2 is deliberately built to be fully valuable under the current statute. The art. 46 exception categories are large, real, and systematically under-served precisely because nobody has removed the notary's justification burden. Bill 34's pullback remains contested inside the profession, and every comparable jurisdiction has moved toward liberalization. If art. 46 loosens, Nota is the only operator already holding the compliance layer, the supply network and the price data. That is upside, not the plan. The plan works if nothing changes.

The grid is data, editable from the operator console without a deploy, and the two lines are frozen on the offer when the client's card is engaged, so a later grid change can never rewrite what an act cost. Two rungs of the date ladder cost Nota money to sell

Moving a refinancing from standard to prioritaire adds 100 $ to Nota's line and 118,90 $ to the Stripe fee — Nota is 18,90 $ worse off on the more urgent act. The same at rapide (+50 $ of revenue against +59,45 $ of fee). Only urgence and extrême pay for the cost they create. This is arithmetic, not opinion, and it is the single most actionable number in the plan: the middle of the urgency ladder — where most urgent demand actually sits — is priced below its own cost of collection. Three remedies exist, all data changes: raise the two lines, add a small percentage component so Nota's line tracks the fee it generates, or move the notary's fee off the platform charge entirely. On the supply side a notary costs about 500 $ to recruit and, at 20 acts a year, returns 3,365 $ of annual gross profit — 10,094 $ over a three-year tenure. LTV to CAC of about 20×, payback inside three acts. Supply, not demand, is where the compounding is.

The repeat-purchase problem, stated plainly

A financing act recurs on the renewal cycle — every five years for a typical fixed-rate mortgage, and sooner when rates move. That is better than the retired will-and-mandate catalogue, where a client bought once a decade, but it is still not a subscription and this plan does not pretend otherwise. Three things carry the business instead.

Supply is the durable asset

Notaries are recurring, not one-time. Every notary recruited compounds; the network is the moat. At payback inside three acts, supply spend is the highest-return line in the budget.

Organic demand capture

The public carnet is a continuously refreshed, hyper-local corpus of real posted prices — structurally the best possible answer to "combien coûte un refinancement chez le notaire à Québec ?". No competitor can index what they do not transact.

Zero-CAC referral

Mortgage brokers above all: the broker sets the rate hold that creates the deadline Nota prices, and has nowhere structured to send a client who needs a notary inside it. Agents and planners sit one step further out on the same path.

Expansion levers, in order of confidence: raise the grid per service and per tier — the most direct lever, and the one the architecture makes free, since the grid is data edited from the console; widen the catalogue, starting with the acte de vente, which rides the same lender deadline; and sell notary practice tooling as a flat subscription for software, never a deduction from an act, which is what makes it safe under art. 32.

Three sensitivities to press on

Volume is the only lever — Nota's price does not scale with the act, so revenue is act count × ~257 $. Halve the count and you halve the revenue; double the average act value and nothing changes except the Stripe bill. The mix moves gross profit far less than it moves the amount transacted — across the whole urgency ladder, gross profit per act stays between 133 $ and 301 $; a pessimistic all-standard mix still yields 166 $. Card processing is the whole cost of revenue and it grows with the notary's fee — every 1,000 $ added to a typical act adds 29 $ to Nota's cost and nothing to its revenue.

What has to be true

Four assumptions carry the model. Each has a named falsification test, and all four resolve inside this round.

Resolves month 6

Clients pay a premium for a date

Test: the realised premium distribution by tier. If urgence and extreme bids do not clear above standard, the core thesis is wrong — and that is visible for well under $100,000.

Resolves month 5

Notaries sell short-notice availability

Test: fill rate on the prioritaire and urgence tiers specifically, not blended.

Resolves at the first fifty bids

Clients pay Nota's price on top of the notary's fee

Test: the abandonment rate at the quote screen, where both lines are shown together before any card is engaged. A different question from the first, and version 1.0 never asked it — a percentage taken out of the notary's fee was invisible to the client.

Resolves month 2

Nota's own price is déontologically safe as structured

Test: a written legal opinion from Québec counsel, with structured engagement with the Chambre des notaires. The direction of the money is already settled and verifiable on the Stripe wire; the qualification is not.

$250,000 is enough to confirm or refute the entire thesis, and not a dollar is spent building something that has not been validated.
